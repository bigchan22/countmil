\section{Experiments}

\subsection{Experimental Setup}

We evaluate aggregate-supervised instance learning under five groups of experiments:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item exactness and runtime benchmarks;
  \item binary MNIST-Bags count supervision;
  \item digit-sum and atomic-support generality benchmarks;
  \item signed Count-MIL with cancellation-heavy bags;
  \item multiclass histogram LLP/PVC comparisons and CIFAR experiments.
\end{enumerate}
Unless otherwise stated, MNIST-family experiments use LeNet-style instance networks, bags are sampled on the fly, and tables report means over three random seeds.
Hidden instance labels are never used for training; they are used only for evaluation.
Column names distinguish training objectives from evaluation metrics: ``Objective'' denotes the loss used for optimization, while count, sum, signed, and histogram errors are mean absolute errors unless explicitly stated otherwise.
Implementation details are summarized in Appendix~\ref{app:implementation-details}.
The experiments are ordered from validation cases and new aggregate types to broader LLP/CIFAR comparisons: binary counts establish equivalence to known count loss; digit-sum, SVHN/UltraMNIST sum, and signed-count tasks test atomic-support generality; histogram and CIFAR experiments position the method against LLP/PVC baselines.

\subsection{Binary CountMIL}

Plain count-likelihood training recovers high hidden-instance AUC across bag sizes.
This validation case verifies that the convolutional formulation behaves like the established Bernoulli count probability while sharing the same implementation pattern as the new aggregate types.

\subsection{Digit-Sum Supervision}

Digit-sum isolates ordinal aggregate supervision because the learner observes a single scalar while the hidden instance label has ten possible values.
Results show that exact finite-support likelihood recovers hidden digit predictors far better than expected-value matching in the low-data small-bag regime.
For MNIST bags with $n=10$, exact likelihood achieves about 98--99\% hidden digit accuracy.
Expected-sum baselines show that matching only the expected aggregate is substantially less effective in the low-data regime, but can become competitive for small bags when the number of training bags increases.

The expected-sum baselines are designed to isolate whether matching only $\sum_i \mathbb{E}[z_i]$ is sufficient.
Results are shown in Table~\ref{tab:digit-sum-baselines}.
For $n=10$ and $1000$ training bags, expected-value matching yields expected-sum MAE around $2.3$--$2.5$ and hidden digit accuracy around $22$--$29\%$, far below the exact likelihood in Table~\ref{tab:digit-sum}.
With $5000$ training bags, however, MAE and Huber baselines improve sharply and recover high instance accuracy for this small-bag setting.
We therefore interpret the result as evidence for the data efficiency of exact scalar-sum likelihood, not as evidence that expected-value baselines universally fail.

\paragraph{Atomic-support generality.}
To check that the same aggregator is not specific to MNIST digit sums, we also evaluate a focused set of variable-size bag benchmarks in which only the atomic support and backbone change.
All use the same finite-support convolutional objective and report the mean over five seeds with a last-five-epoch selector.
MNIST-sum uses $S=10$ digit atoms on clean handwritten digits; SVHN-sum uses the same $S=10$ ordinal support on natural RGB house-number digits; UltraMNIST uses small RGB digit patches with bag sizes $3$--$5$; and MNIST-signed uses $S=3$ signed atoms.
Table~\ref{tab:atomic-support-generality} reports the \method{} row for each aggregate-support variant.
The results support the intended interpretation of Eq.~\eqref{eq:aggregate-support-width}: changing $S$ and the contribution values creates a new sum-label problem without changing the aggregate likelihood layer.

\subsection{FashionMNIST Robustness}

FashionMNIST confirms that attention baselines can achieve strong bag prediction while providing much weaker hidden-instance recovery than count likelihood training.
Binary CountMIL achieves hidden-instance AUC around $0.997$--$0.998$, while attention and gated-attention baselines reach strong bag prediction but only about $0.70$--$0.74$ instance AUC.
Signed CountMIL also preserves high instance AUC around $0.996$--$0.998$.
Digit-sum supervision is less informative on FashionMNIST, whereas histogram LLP provides stronger category recovery.
We therefore use FashionMNIST primarily as a robustness evaluation for instance recovery.

\subsection{Histogram LLP and PVC}

Histogram supervision is much richer than scalar sums, and LLP baselines are strong on MNIST-family data.
We use these experiments to clarify where exact aggregate likelihood adds value and where existing LLP-style objectives are already competitive.
Table~\ref{tab:histogram-llp-pvc} reports the histogram results.
KL and CE proportion matching are strong, but the one-vs-rest count likelihood/PVC objective consistently improves both hidden digit recovery and histogram count MAE.
The advantage is clearest for larger bags: at $n=50$ and $1000$ training bags, PVC improves digit accuracy from $96.4\%$ to $98.5\%$ and reduces count MAE from $0.376$ to $0.137$.
MSE proportion matching is not competitive for large bags.

\subsection{CIFAR Experiments}

CIFAR-10 with ResNet-18 is a protocol-sensitive comparison setting for LLP-PVC, not the main evidence for the finite-support generalization.
Ma et al. report CIFAR-10 results with a ResNet-18 backbone, and their appendix specifies ImageNet-pretrained initialization.
We therefore evaluate both randomly initialized and ImageNet-pretrained ResNet-18 backbones because the representation source has a large effect on LLP performance.
The from-scratch setting evaluates the same pipeline without external pretrained features, while the pretrained setting matches the backbone initialization specified for LLP-PVC.

The from-scratch results are substantially below the LLP-PVC reference, showing that the CIFAR comparison is not meaningful unless the backbone initialization protocol is matched.
With ImageNet-pretrained ResNet-18 and the PVC objective, the $n=16$ and $n=64$ settings reach about $90.2\%$ and $91.4\%$ final instance accuracy, respectively, above the LLP-PVC reference accuracies for those bag sizes.
However, the $n=64$, train $1000$ setting exposes four times as many image instances per epoch as $n=16$, train $1000$.
The fixed-instance-budget setting, $n=64$ with $250$ train bags, reaches only about $80.3\%$ final instance accuracy and $80.6\%$ when selecting by observed histogram MAE.
Thus the CIFAR-10 results support the usefulness of pretrained PVC/count likelihood under a strong representation, but they also show that the apparent $n=64$ gain comes from greater total image exposure or aggregate observations rather than bag size alone.
They do not justify a claim that larger bags are intrinsically easier or more scalable at fixed image-instance exposure.

The CIFAR-100 coarse experiment used 20 coarse classes, bag size $50$, train bags $1000$, and two seeds.
PVC and KL reach about $30\%$ hidden coarse-class accuracy, above the $5\%$ random baseline, but this setting remains exploratory rather than a primary claim.
We therefore use CIFAR-10 for direct LLP-PVC comparison and treat CIFAR-100 as an exploratory higher-class-count setting.

\subsection{Runtime}

Our FFT-tree backend computes batched multiclass one-vs-rest count PMFs on GPU and gives large practical speedups over CPU dynamic programming in large-bag benchmarks.
This addresses a practical limitation of DP-based count losses: exact count probabilities need not be tied to a sequential CPU implementation.
The benchmark isolates aggregate-PMF evaluation and should be read as backend evidence, not as an end-to-end training-speed claim or as evidence about representation quality.
